Purpose · Why this approach

Building exemplary public institutions — by design

Government has the ambition, the mandate, and the people. What this approach adds is the structural discipline to turn that ambition into measurable citizen outcomes: every investment traceable to evidence, every rand tied to a mandate commitment, every roadmap tested against real delivery capacity before it begins.

"True digital transformation serves as the vehicle for building exemplary, high-performing public institutions. Far beyond a mere technological upgrade, it is a holistic practice that leverages sound enterprise architecture, a prepared enabling environment, and robust digital public infrastructure to drive comprehensive organisational evolution, heightened public trust, and inclusive performance excellence."

— Purpose of Digital Transformation and EA · GWEA 2.0 framework

The opportunity

Digital transformation gives government a once-in-a-generation opportunity to build delivery capacity that scales — where a citizen's record follows them seamlessly across departments, where services are available anywhere, and where every rand spent on technology can be traced directly to an improved outcome. Achieving that requires something more than technology projects: it requires architecture, readiness, and a shared understanding of what good looks like. That is exactly what this framework provides.

Why it matters now — the cost of leaving this to chance

Departments already carry the consequences of an un-architected estate, and they carry them in public. Audit findings recur year after year because the same data is captured in three systems and reconciles in none. Conditional grants go unspent because a project could not be procured, staffed and delivered inside the financial year. Parliamentary questions take weeks to answer because no one holds a single authoritative record. Annual Performance Plan targets are missed, and the department cannot say precisely which capability gap caused the miss — which means the following year's plan repeats the same assumption. None of this is a technology failure. It is the absence of a shared, evidence-based picture of what the department has, what it is worth, and what to fix first. That picture is what this method produces, and it is the same picture the Auditor-General, National Treasury and the Presidency are increasingly asking departments to show.

The layered model — read upward and downward

Every element of government delivery is connected. The levels below enable the level above. Read upward to understand what makes success possible. Read downward to understand which specific element to strengthen when a target needs to improve.

These four levels describe how the framework fits together. They are not the five ArchiMate architecture layers (Motivation, Strategy, Business, IS Solution, Technology) used from Phase 1 onward to classify a department's own catalogue items — that is a different model, one level down.

HARD GATE · The foundation of credible delivery

Dimension 11 — Change & Delivery Capability

This enabling-environment dimension must reach Established (level 3) before the architecture roadmap is approved for execution — because a department with strong delivery capability multiplies the value of every investment it makes. Building this capability first is not a constraint; it is the single most important accelerant in the whole framework.

Departments that invest in delivery muscle — a functioning PMO, experienced programme managers, a track record of completing projects on scope and on time — get dramatically more value from every rand spent on technology and architecture. The gate exists to secure that advantage before anything ambitious begins.

What makes this approach distinctive

Diagnosis before prescription

Every improvement opportunity is traced to a named, scored architectural element before any investment is proposed. The business case is built on evidence — not estimates — which means approval decisions are well-founded and benefits are verifiable.

Evidence-based business cases

Business cases are anchored in objective baseline scores, named elements, and projected score improvements. Approval committees can interrogate the logic, compare options, and track delivery — turning investment governance into a genuine value conversation.

Delivery capability built first

The hard gate ensures that delivery muscle is developed as the first investment — so when the ambitious architecture work begins, the department is genuinely ready to execute it. Capability before complexity.

Enabling environment as first-class

Policy, procurement agility, data stewardship and executive sponsorship are assessed and improved in parallel with the technology architecture. Readiness is built alongside the plan, not discovered as a gap after go-live.

A repeatable improvement cycle

GWEA 2.0 is a five-phase cycle tied to the government planning calendar. Each cycle produces a refreshed baseline, an updated roadmap, and measured benefits from the previous cycle. Improvement compounds over time.

Shared infrastructure, greater reach

The technology layer is built toward Digital Public Infrastructure — national shared platforms for identity, payments and data exchange. Departments progressively stop duplicating capability in isolation, and the savings in cost and complexity are reinvested into citizen outcomes.

What this asks of a department

The method is deliberately built so that the first step is small, cheap and reversible. A department does not commit to a programme; it commits to establishing an honest baseline, and decides what to do next once it can see the picture.

Step one — a two-hour conversation

Phase 0 is a 30-question maturity questionnaire across six dimensions, completed in a single structured interview with the department's ICT, planning and M&E leads. It produces a scored baseline against the 3.0 target and a written finding per dimension. Nothing else is required to start.

What the department supplies

Documents the department already has and already tables: the Annual Performance Plan, the Service Delivery Model, OMF process maps, the ICT Plan (PSR Reg 93), the application and asset registers, and existing data-sharing agreements. No new documentation is written for the assessment.

What it costs in time

Phase 0 is one interview. A full Phase 1–2 catalogue and assessment runs over several weeks alongside normal work, drawing mainly on the ICT, planning and programme functions. Phases 3–5 are tied to the existing planning calendar rather than adding a new one.

What the department holds afterwards

A complete catalogue of 15 architecture elements, an objective score with a named binding constraint per element, an eleven-dimension readiness profile, a sequenced three-horizon roadmap, and a standing set of stakeholder artefacts — from the Architecture Health Report to the Strategic Investment Case — that can be tabled to executive committee, Treasury and the Auditor-General.
